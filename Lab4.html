Question 1¶

I plotted the particle counts as a function of time, solar phase, and Earth longitude to look for patterns in the data. Most measurements contain roughly 3–13 particles per 100 ms, but the average background is not constant. The average count changes with solar phase and longitude, ranging from approximately 3 to 13 particles per 100 ms.

The time plots also show brief periods of elevated particle counts that repeat approximately every 5,400 seconds, which matches the satellite's 90-minute orbital period. This suggests that much of the variation is related to the satellite's orbit rather than random fluctuations alone.

There is some potential for signal contamination in the data, since unusually high particle counts could include gamma-ray bursts. However, the regularly repeating increases are more likely associated with the changing background than with individual bursts. I cannot identify any specific GRBs from these plots alone.

To construct a background probability distribution, I plan to use a Poisson distribution as a starting point, since we are counting individual particle detections. Because the background changes with time, I would estimate the expected count at different points within the approximately 5,400-second orbit rather than assuming one constant mean.

Question 2¶

The background varies periodically with the satellite's orbit. After averaging the particle counts into one-minute intervals, I found that the background follows a repeating pattern approximately every 5,400 seconds, or 90 minutes. The average count gradually decreases from approximately 11 to 6 particles per 100 milliseconds, then rises sharply back to about 11 before gradually decreasing again. The pattern repeats consistently over the five orbits shown in the plot. This suggests that the background is strongly influenced by the satellite's orbital motion.

Question 4¶

The threshold varies from approximately 22 particles per 100 milliseconds during the lowest-background portion of the orbit to 32 particles during the highest-background portion. The threshold follows the same general pattern as the expected background, decreasing gradually before increasing sharply around 4,000 seconds into the orbit. This demonstrates why a constant detection threshold would be inappropriate. An observation that qualifies as a five-sigma detection during a low-background period might not be significant during a high-background period.

Question 5¶

My initial thought is that the background variation could be partially caused by Earth blocking radiation from the Sun or other regions of the sky as the satellite orbits. The gradual decrease in particle counts followed by a sharp increase could indicate a changing amount of radiation reaching the detector as a source becomes obscured and later reappears. However, cosmic rays arrive approximately isotropically, making the occultation of a single source less likely.

Another possible explanation is the interaction of charged cosmic rays with Earth's magnetic field. The magnetic field can deflect incoming charged particles, and the effectiveness of this shielding varies with the satellite's position. The South Atlantic Anomaly is a region where Earth's magnetic field is relatively weak, allowing energetic charged particles from Earth's radiation belts to reach lower altitudes.

I propose that the satellite's changing exposure to these particles contributes to the periodic variation in its background count rate. The data show a much sharper transition than one would expect from a magnetic-field structure, suggesting that the effect may have been simplified for the simulation.
